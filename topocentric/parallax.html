What Is Parallax?

The street sign illusion: why where you stand changes the sky.

| The Street Sign

Imagine you are in the passenger seat of a car, parked at the curb, looking out of your window at a street sign on the sidewalk. Across the street, behind the sign, is a row of buildings.

From your seat the sign lines up perfectly with a blue front door on the far side of the street. Now ask the driver what they see. The driver is sitting a few feet to your left, so their line of sight is different. To the driver, the very same sign lines up with a red brick chimney further down the block.

The sign did not move. The buildings did not move. You and the driver simply looked from two different places, and so the sign appeared to sit in front of two different backgrounds. That apparent shift is called parallax.

Try it now

Hold a finger up at arm's length and look at it against the far wall. Close your left eye, then your right. Your finger jumps sideways against the wall. Your two eyes are only a few centimeters apart, and that is enough. The closer the finger, the bigger the jump.

| The Moon Is Our Street Sign

The same thing happens in the sky, every second of every day. The Moon is the closest thing to us, so it plays the street sign. The distant stars, and the 64 Gates of the Human Design wheel laid over them, play the row of buildings.

Two observers on opposite sides of the Earth are about 12,742 km apart, and the Moon is about 384,400 km away. That is like two people about one meter apart looking at a sign 30 meters down the street. The shift is small, but it is real and it is measurable: seen from two distant places at the same instant, the Moon sits against the background sky up to about 1.0 degree apart, roughly twice the Moon's own width.

London and Sydney

If someone in London and someone in Sydney look at the Moon at exactly the same second, they see it against slightly different background stars. Neither of them is wrong. They are the passenger and the driver.

There is one more twist. The shift is biggest when the Moon is low near the horizon and shrinks to almost nothing when the Moon is straight overhead. So the shift depends on where you are and on the time of day, because time decides where the Moon sits in your sky.

| Where a Geocentric Chart Stands

Traditional astrology software, and the Human Design software built on it, calculates every planet from one vantage point: the exact center of the Earth, about 6,371 km below your feet. That is what geocentric means. It is as if everybody who has ever been born stood in the same room at the center of the planet and looked up together.

Calculated that way, parallax cannot exist, because there is only one observer. If a program does not know where on the surface you actually stood, it cannot tell you where the Moon actually was from your line of sight. It can only tell you where the Moon was from the middle of the Earth.

For a slow, distant planet the difference between the center of the Earth and your birthplace is too small to matter in any system. For the Moon, in a system as finely divided as Human Design, it can be a whole Line.

| Where a Topocentric Chart Stands

Topocentric means calculated from your actual coordinates on the surface: your latitude, your longitude and your height above sea level. Genetic Matrix calculates the sky as you would have seen it, not as the center of the Earth would have.

Because the Moon moves quickly, about 0.55 degrees an hour, a one-degree parallax difference is the same as the Moon being almost two hours ahead of or behind its geocentric position. In Human Design that is more than a Line, and it is dozens of Tones. | Frequently Asked Questions

Is parallax a theory or a measurement?
A measurement, and a very old one. Astronomers have used the Moon's parallax since antiquity to work out how far away it is, and every modern ephemeris, the Swiss Ephemeris included, can calculate positions for a specific point on the Earth's surface. Topocentric is the standard name for that calculation.
Does parallax affect every planet?
Every body, but by very different amounts. The Moon shifts by up to about 1.0 degree because it is close. The Sun shifts by under 9 arc-seconds. The planets sit in between, measured in arc-seconds rather than degrees. In Human Design terms the Moon can move a whole Line; everything else moves at most a Base or a Tone.
Why does the time of day matter?
Because parallax is largest when a body is near the horizon and close to zero when it is straight overhead. Two people in the same city born twelve hours apart see the Moon in different parts of the sky, so the parallax shift on their Moons is different too. Place decides which sky you had; time decides where the Moon was in it.
Does this mean geocentric charts are inaccurate?
Geocentric charts are exactly what they claim to be: the sky as seen from the center of the Earth. They are accurate to that vantage point. What they leave out is the difference between that vantage point and yours. For a slow planet the difference is nothing. For the Moon it can be a Line.
